# Creil Court Orders Al-Ghazali Muslim School Reopened, Overturning State Closure

The administrative court in Amiens suspended on Friday, November 6 the prefect's order that had closed the Al-Ghazali Muslim private school group in Creil for six months, according to Saphirnews reporting.

What happened

The Oise prefect had issued a closure order on September 29, which forced approximately 259 students at Al-Ghazali—enrolled from primary through secondary level—out of their classrooms at the end of September. The administrative court's suspension order means students will be permitted to return to classes as of Monday, November 9, marking a reversal of a closure that lasted only forty days rather than the originally prescribed six months.

This judicial decision represents a significant reversal: the same court had validated the prefect's closure order approximately one month earlier, according to Saphirnews. The court's latest ruling effectively overturns that earlier judgment, allowing the school to resume operations.

Why it matters

The case reflects broader tensions in France over state authority regarding religious education and the regulation of private Muslim schools. The prefect's office in Oise maintained pressure on Al-Ghazali, yet the administrative court's decision to suspend the closure order signals judicial skepticism toward the state's grounds for the extended shutdown. For the Al-Ghazali community and Muslim education advocates in France, the ruling demonstrates that courts may check executive action even after initially supporting it. For the broader French public, the case illustrates ongoing disputes over how state authorities balance secularism and institutional oversight with parents' rights to religious schooling.

Background

Al-Ghazali operates as a private Muslim school group in Creil, an industrial city in the Oise department north of Paris. France's education system includes both public institutions governed by strict secular principles and private schools, including religious establishments, which operate under different regulatory frameworks while remaining subject to state oversight. The prefect—the state's representative in each department—holds authority to issue administrative orders affecting local institutions, though such decisions remain subject to review by administrative courts.
